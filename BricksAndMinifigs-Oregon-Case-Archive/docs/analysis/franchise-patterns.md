# Franchise Pattern Analysis  
*Documented patterns across Bricks & Minifigs franchise locations, with emphasis on Oregon incidents*

This document examines recurring patterns reported across multiple Bricks & Minifigs (BAM) franchise locations, with a focus on Oregon stores.  
It does not assert wrongdoing. Instead, it identifies **consistent behaviors, operational issues, and structural weaknesses** that appear across multiple incidents — including the Keizer consignment case.

The purpose of this analysis is to help investigators, journalists, and community members understand whether the Keizer incident is isolated or part of a broader systemic pattern.

---

# 🧱 1. Overview of the Franchise Structure

Bricks & Minifigs operates as a **franchise network**, meaning:

- Individual stores are owned by franchisees  
- Corporate controls branding, standards, and franchise agreements  
- Corporate can seize stores for contractual violations  
- Inventory ownership varies by location  
- Consignment practices are not standardized  

This decentralized structure creates **inconsistent handling of consigned property**, especially during ownership transitions or corporate seizures.

---

# 🧱 2. Documented Patterns Across Oregon Locations

Multiple Oregon franchisees — including Keizer and Canby — have reported similar issues during corporate interventions.  
The following patterns are based on:

- Public statements  
- Franchisee accounts  
- Customer reports  
- Evidence archived in this repository  
- Media coverage  
- Witness summaries  

These patterns do not imply intent. They highlight recurring operational outcomes.

---

# 🧩 Pattern 1: Corporate Seizure of Franchise Locations

Several Oregon stores experienced sudden corporate takeovers where:

- Corporate staff entered the store  
- Franchise owners were removed  
- Inventory was seized  
- Operations were transferred to a new operator  

During these seizures, **consigned property was not clearly separated from store inventory**, creating disputes over ownership.

This occurred in:

- **Keizer, Oregon**  
- **Canby, Oregon**  
- Additional stores reported similar concerns privately  

---

# 🧩 Pattern 2: Disputed Inventory Handling

Across multiple incidents, franchisees and consignors reported:

- Missing inventory  
- Items being sold without proper tracking  
- Removal of identifying labels  
- Lack of documentation during transitions  
- Conflicting explanations from corporate or new operators  

In the Keizer case, evidence shows:

- Labels removed from consigned LEGO sets  
- Items moved or reorganized  
- Refusal to allow inspection of the back room  
- Continued sales of consigned items after the takeover  

These behaviors align with reports from other Oregon locations.

---

# 🧩 Pattern 3: Conflicting Statements from Corporate

In several cases, corporate representatives:

- Acknowledged consignment agreements verbally  
- Later denied responsibility  
- Provided inconsistent explanations  
- Declared matters “closed” without resolution  
- Failed to follow through on promised inventory reviews  

In the Keizer case, corporate was recorded stating:

> “Brandon will be taking over the consignment as well.”

Yet later communications contradicted this.

---

# 🧩 Pattern 4: Franchisee Complaints About Corporate Conduct

Former franchisees have publicly described:

- Sudden contract terminations  
- Lack of support  
- Aggressive enforcement actions  
- Inventory disputes  
- Financial pressure  
- Poor communication  

Some franchisees reported losing personal or consigned inventory during corporate interventions.

These accounts mirror the Keizer incident.

---

# 🧩 Pattern 5: Lack of Standardized Consignment Protocols

Across the BAM network, consignment practices vary widely:

- Some stores use detailed tracking systems  
- Others rely on handwritten notes  
- Some provide monthly payouts  
- Others pay irregularly  
- Labeling systems differ by location  

This inconsistency creates vulnerabilities when:

- Ownership changes  
- Corporate seizes a store  
- Inventory is reorganized  
- Staff turnover occurs  

The Keizer case demonstrates how these inconsistencies can lead to disputes.

---

# 🧩 Pattern 6: Legal Cost Barriers Prevent Resolution

In multiple incidents, consignors or franchisees reported:

- High legal costs  
- Difficulty obtaining police intervention  
- Corporate refusing mediation  
- Cases becoming financially impossible to pursue  

In the Keizer case, attorneys estimated **$60,000+** for litigation — a cost barrier that effectively prevents recovery.

This pattern appears across other disputes in the BAM network.

---

# 🧱 3. Why These Patterns Matter

These patterns suggest structural issues within the franchise system:

### ✔️ Lack of standardized consignment procedures  
### ✔️ Poor documentation during corporate seizures  
### ✔️ Inconsistent communication from corporate  
### ✔️ Vulnerability of consignors during ownership transitions  
### ✔️ High legal barriers preventing resolution  
### ✔️ Repeated disputes across multiple Oregon locations  

These issues do not prove wrongdoing.  
They highlight **systemic weaknesses** that can lead to disputes like the Keizer case.

The Keizer incident shares multiple characteristics with other reported disputes within the Bricks & Minifigs franchise network.  
While each case is unique, the recurring patterns suggest systemic vulnerabilities that warrant further investigation.

This document does not assign blame.  
It provides a structured, evidence‑based overview of patterns that may help investigators, journalists, and community members understand the broader context.
