# Legal Analysis  
*An evidence‑based breakdown of the legal issues surrounding the Bricks & Minifigs Keizer consignment case*

This document provides a structured, neutral analysis of the legal issues raised by the seizure, handling, and disputed sale of a large LEGO Star Wars collection consigned to the Bricks & Minifigs (BAM) store in Keizer, Oregon.  
It does not assert legal conclusions. Instead, it examines how Oregon law, consignment rules, and franchise obligations intersect with the documented events.

---

# ⚖️ 1. Ownership and Consignment Law

## 1.1. Consigned Property Remains the Owner’s Property  
Under Oregon law, consignment does **not** transfer ownership.  
The consignor retains full legal title unless a written agreement explicitly states otherwise.

Evidence in this case shows:

- A signed consignment contract  
- Monthly payouts for over a year  
- Receipts documenting ongoing sales  
- Inventory lists maintained by the store  
- Public display and promotion of the collection  

These facts strongly indicate that the LEGO collection remained the property of the consignor at all times.

---

# ⚖️ 2. Potential Civil Violations

## 2.1. Conversion (Civil Theft)  
**Conversion** occurs when someone intentionally exercises control over property belonging to another, without permission, and in a way that interferes with the owner’s rights.

Documented actions that may fall under conversion include:

- Removing identifying labels from consigned items  
- Refusing to return property upon demand  
- Continuing to sell consigned items after the takeover  
- Denying access to the back room where items were stored  
- Providing conflicting explanations for missing inventory  

If proven, conversion can result in damages equal to the value of the property plus additional penalties.

---

## 2.2. Breach of Contract  
The original consignment agreement required:

- Proper handling of the collection  
- Accurate tracking of sales  
- Monthly payouts  
- Return of unsold items upon request  

Failure to uphold these obligations may constitute breach of contract by:

- The original franchise owner (Chrystal Law / Gorman)  
- The new operator (Brandon Best)  
- Potentially Bricks & Minifigs corporate, depending on their role  

---

## 2.3. Breach of Bailment  
A **bailment** occurs when property is entrusted to another party for a specific purpose.  
The bailee (the store) must:

- Safeguard the property  
- Return it upon demand  
- Not use it for unauthorized purposes  

If the property is lost, damaged, or sold without permission, the bailee may be liable.

# ⚖️ 3. Potential Criminal Issues

## 3.1. Theft by Receiving / Theft by Deception  
Oregon law includes provisions for:

- **Theft by receiving** (possessing property known to be stolen)  
- **Theft by deception** (obtaining property through false statements)  

If a party knowingly sold or withheld consigned property while denying the owner’s rights, these statutes may be relevant.

Police classified the matter as civil, but the documented behavior overlaps with elements of criminal statutes.

---

## 3.2. Tampering with Evidence or Records  
Removing labels from consigned items may raise questions about:

- Intent  
- Knowledge of ownership  
- Efforts to obscure provenance  

While not conclusive, this behavior is relevant to legal analysis.

# ⚖️ 4. Franchise Law Considerations

## 4.1. Corporate Seizure of a Franchise  
When Bricks & Minifigs corporate seized the Keizer store, they assumed control of:

- Inventory  
- Operations  
- Customer relationships  
- Physical assets  

The key legal question:  
**Did corporate also assume responsibility for consigned property?**

Evidence includes:

- Recorded audio of corporate stating the new operator would “take over the consignment”  
- Corporate’s refusal to return items  
- Corporate’s later claim that the matter was “closed”  

These conflicting actions raise questions about corporate liability.

---

## 4.2. Pattern of Behavior  
Other Oregon franchisees, including the Canby location, have reported:

- Corporate seizures  
- Disputed inventory handling  
- Loss of consigned or privately owned items  

If multiple incidents share similar characteristics, this may indicate systemic issues within the franchise network.

# ⚖️ 5. Law Enforcement Response

Police acknowledged:

- The existence of a consignment agreement  
- The presence of documentation  
- The removal of labels  
- The refusal to return property  

However, they classified the matter as a **civil dispute**, advising the family to pursue litigation.

This is common in consignment cases, even when criminal elements may be present.

# ⚖️ 6. Barriers to Legal Action

Attorneys estimated:

- **$60,000+** to pursue an injunction and full litigation  
- Additional costs for expert witnesses, discovery, and depositions  
- A multi‑year timeline  

For many families, this cost is prohibitive — effectively preventing legal recourse.

This financial barrier is a recurring theme in consignment disputes.
